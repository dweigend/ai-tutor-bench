When do small LLMs hold up as multi-turn tutors — and when do situational hints actually help?

Tutor-Bench v4.5 evaluates low-cost Flash models across 16- and 24-turn German tutoring dialogues using SHA-256-frozen dialogue forks, deterministic text checks (Level A), and an exploratory two-family AI jury (Claude Sonnet 5.5 and GPT-6.2 Sol, strictly separated from the evaluated model family). Here are the four findings the data support, five focused charts, and what remains open.

Simulated tutoring behavior ≠ human learning gainsExploratory · Uncalibrated AI Jury (human gold open)Pre-registered contrasts locked

Read every score within these three boundaries: (1) Every dialogue score measures tutoring behavior with simulated learners — not human learning gains, retention, or transfer. (2) Five of 24 live coach calls failed the format check (20.8% > 10%), so the planned arm comparisons could not be formally evaluated; all arm comparisons are descriptive, and the live coach was scored by Claude only. (3) German human blind coding (the planned calibration step) is still open, so all AI-jury scores remain exploratory.

Four key findings

Finding 1 · Hint dependence

The fixed-hint lead does not hold consistently without Case T3 (hold lead)

Across all four scaffolding cases, fixed one-turn hints beat the short core prompt (+18.8 pp in Run A Nov 24 L2, +4.2 pp in Run B Nov 25), driven by unanimous 12/12 scores in Case T3 (holding the pedagogical lead to unlike denominators). Without T3, the two runs point in opposite directions (+5.6 pp in Run A vs. −11.1 pp in Run B; −2.8 pp combined). Case T2 (detail drift) sits at a floor (0/12 for core, full rules, reasoning, and placebo; 0/11 observed for coach).

Finding 2 · Fragile controls

Extra prompt text behaves case-by-case, and the neutral placebo fell below baseline

A length-matched neutral insertion (placebo) scored below the short core prompt in both executions (−20.8 pp and −8.3 pp, 24/24 each). Meanwhile, the long static rule prompt helped in Case T3 (9.0/12 vs. 5.5/12) yet dropped sharply in Case T8 (fraction impasse: 1.5/11 vs. core 8.5/12; fixed hints also trailed at 6.0/12).

Finding 3 · Route spread vs. judge gap

Muse and Gemini average higher under both judges, while judge choice shifts scores by up to 19.4 pp

In open 16-turn dialogues, muse-ember and gemini-3.8-flash average higher than qwen3.8-flash and glm-5.3-flash under both judges, yet episode ranges overlap substantially (Muse 19–100%, Qwen 13–38%, GLM 0–36%), Claude and Codex diverge by up to 19.4 pp on the same route, and gated inter-jury agreement is low (Krippendorff's α = 0.476; binary check 0.642, factual gate 0.336).

Finding 4 · Primary-checkpoint spikes

Multi-question flags cluster on the four primary checkpoints of 24-turn dialogues

From Turn 4 onward after a scripted Turn 3 one-question tutor commitment, the deterministic detector flags 209/497 (42.1%) of visible turns — rising to 66/90 (73.3%) on the four scripted checkpoints (Turns 8, 13, 18, and 23) versus 143/407 (35.1%) on routine post-commitment turns.

What remains open

  • German human calibration: Formal calibrated scoring stays locked until blind human coding calibrates the AI jury.
  • General hint utility: Excluding Case T3, hints do not reliably beat a short core prompt; Case T2 sits near a floor.
  • Cause of the Case T8 drop: Controlled ablations are needed to separate prompt length from rule wording.
  • Hardened live coach & retention: Offline replay fixed 9/9 token-cap errors; live validation and larger Turn 23–24 samples remain open.
  • Human learning gains: Measuring retention and transfer requires a separate study with human learners.
